MARCO · a sense of place

Where is it, though?

45 statements, an address you can say out loud, and a root you can recompute.

A building is not a place

A person says "I'm in the kitchen" and you already know what she can see, who is with her, what she can reach, and what happens next. Nothing in that sentence is a coordinate.

Ask a program the same question and it answers room-2041, or floor 2, or a latitude. Each of those names a building, or a point inside one.

A building is not a place. A place is what its occupant can see, reach, change and expect, and none of that survives the name.

the same sentence, two answers

  • a person a kitchen, a window, a friend, the middle of cooking. Break something and it stays broken.
  • a program room-2041, floor 2, building C.

This is a distillation of two finished strands

Compression: what is actually new here

The parent project's literature survey found six of the ten components of this design are textbook: Geohash, proquints, locality-sensitive hashing, Merkle commitments, metric embeddings and CCA.

The defensible novelty is narrow. This address is pronounceable and Gray-coded and locality-preserving at once, and the three are held together by one codebook. Geohash is locality-preserving but not pronounceable; proquints are pronounceable but not locality-preserving.

3,300 bytes in · 67,561-byte frame out · 20.47x

Self-generation: a generator, not a signal inventing a decoder

The parent project's open question 003 asked whether a signal could teach its own decoder. It concluded that with literally zero prior knowledge self-bootstrap is impossible, and that what is coherent is a fixed minimal interpreter plus a generator.

That is what runs here. The seed holds no questions and no situations, only a generator that picks the next question by how evenly it splits the surviving candidates.

64 of 64 trials resolved · mean 17.8 questions · floor log2(204) = 7.67

The honest half: it is a generator over a given population. It does not invent a decoder from nothing.

rung 2

Five markers, and the weakest one wins

Every statement arrives with a marker, and the five markers sit on a ladder. The seed carries all five with their ranks. Only the top two can hold a claim.

  • =
    anchorschecked and matched exactly
  • ⊢
    anchorsworked out the same way twice from what I was given
  • ≈
    no anchormeasured with an instrument, so roughly
  • ~
    no anchormodel belief, not evidence
  • ?
    no anchorunsupported guess

One ~ anywhere in the input drops the whole reading to ~. The rule is a single function, weakest(), in marco/locus.py. The kitchen with a friend carries ≈; the locked storeroom carries ⊢.

weakest present wins · ≈ never anchors · the marker only ever falls

rung 3

Both sides need the same frame, and it fits in 3,300 bytes

Two agents can compare coordinates only if they build the same frame: the same 45 statements, the same syllable codebook, the same axis order, the same distance table, the same marker ladder.

The usual answer is to transmit that frame, and it costs 67,561 bytes. Every one of those bytes is derived from something smaller.

The answer here is to transmit the smaller thing. The seed goes out; the receiver rebuilds a 4,532-byte frame that would have cost 67,561 bytes to ship. Both sides then compare one fingerprint before anything else.

the unroll

From the seed to an address and a root, in eight stages

Eight stages, one per screenful. The values are the real intermediate values for the kitchen with a friend, whose 18 true statements produce the address and the root at the end.

stage 1 of 8

One small file

The seed is the whole shared substrate. It carries no situations and no questions, and it is small on purpose.

stage 2 of 8

Forty-five statements come out of it

Each key is a namespace, a relation and a value, and each one carries its plain reading. The register below is the same 45 cells as before, numbered 1 to 45.

45 statements · each with its plain reading · nothing ticked

stage 3 of 8

The occupant reports which ones are true

Eighteen of the 45 come back true. The first three are change/this_place/any, know/where_i_am/any and know/who_else_is_here/any, which in the primer's own words read "I can change things here", "I know where I am" and "I know who else is here".

18 of 45 true · filled cells are the reading

stage 4 of 8

Each statement becomes a distance

One number per statement, drawn from four levels: 0.0 when the statement is the axis itself, 0.3 for the same group and subject, 0.6 for the same group, 0.9 for a different group. This reading produces 18 cells at 0.0 and 27 at 0.6.

45 numbers · 0.0 x18, 0.6 x27 · the other two levels do not occur here

stage 5 of 8

Two bits per axis, in a derived order

Four branches per axis means two bits, so 45 axes become 90 bits. They are written in the derived axis order, which is why the axis numbers underneath are not 0, 1, 2, 3.

90 bits · 2 per axis · the order is a permutation of 0 to 44

stage 6 of 8

Every five bits become one syllable

The 90 bits are cut into 18 chunks of five, each chunk is Gray-decoded, and the result indexes a codebook of 32 syllables. Neighbours in that codebook differ by one phoneme, so one changed bit changes one sound.

18 chunks of 5 bits · 18 syllables · 32 in the codebook

stage 7 of 8

The syllables become four beats

A dot stays inside a beat, a middle dot separates place from what, a plus separates what from reach, and a tilde opens the last beat. Similar places read alike, and that proves nothing about identity.

⌁ ma.mo.ma.ma.ru · ma.me.ki.va.ki + ve.ki.va.tu.ru ~ ki.ru.ki

place · what + reach ~ now · 18 syllables · 4 beats

stage 8 of 8

The statements become a root

The same 18 statements go through one hash and 64 hex characters come out. Add a single pinned timestamp to the set and the second hash is unrelated to the first, while the address above does not move a syllable.

the same 18 statements · two different roots · one statement different is an unrelated root

Why six systems could agree at all

Handing six systems the same 45 statements does not explain the result. A shared vocabulary tells you what to answer with; it does not make two differently built systems put the same situation in the same part of their own space. If that were all that was happening, the addresses would be arbitrary but consistent, like two people agreeing to call a colour "seven".

The result implies something stronger. Situations appear to occupy a structure that independently trained systems have in common, and the primer is a chart laid over that structure rather than a substitute for it.

The two halves are different kinds of object. The manifold is shared, emergent and not ours: we did not build it, we cannot inspect it directly, and its existence is inferred from the fact that independent systems agree. The chart is ours, published and small: 45 statements, a 32-syllable codebook, an axis order derived over 204 places, one hashing rule, 3,300 bytes.

the manifold is inferred from agreement · the chart is published and 3,300 bytes

The same grid, the same mountain

Hand two cartographers the same grid and they will label the same mountain the same way, and that agreement is only worth something because the mountain was there first. The grid is ours. The mountain is not.

Where it stops: the chart is shared too, but only when the states are unambiguous. Ambiguous states send the charts apart again, which is why the six places on this page are described in such plain, concrete English. The honest version of the claim is that the layout agreement holds for clearly described situations.

The parent project reached this conclusion and then corrected itself on the follow-up question: one of its own attempts to measure the chart put its control above its treatment, which invalidated that comparison. This is that finding restated, inferred from agreement rather than observed directly.

What this run does not show

The shared-vocabulary caveat is inherited, not discovered here. The parent experiment's handover report states it first: its blind cross-model matching used a shared anchor vocabulary and a deterministic encoder, so it demonstrates encoding-level matching given a shared primer. It does not establish independence from a shared ontology. Its recommended follow-up, a genuine-versus-shuffled-anchors-versus-random-loci-versus-garbage study, has still not been run.

The parent project also set out the falsifier. Independence from a shared ontology is shown only when genuine anchors beat shuffled anchors, syntax-valid random loci, and an empty or garbage condition, not when a shared-label run scores well. This repository restates the caveat because it still applies and nothing here removes it.

What this repository does add is one control the parent run did not have. A random-vocabulary null at equal information content replaces each family's selection with a random set of the same size drawn from the same 45 statements. That null scores 20.9% against the real 96.7%, which shows the content of the choices carries the signal rather than merely the size of the sets.

That narrows the caveat without removing it. The families still chose from a vocabulary we handed them, so what is shown is that the frame is usable by minds with nothing in common, not that minds with different vocabularies would invent the same one.

They agreed on 88.8% of their statement choices, and the addresses inherit that agreement. The warehouse alone is the worst case: 5 of 6 families produced five different statement sets and five different roots, and only 2 landed on the same root. The locked storeroom and the dark empty room are the best, at 5 of 6 each.

No claim is made about meaning, understanding or intent. The address is descriptive and never authoritative. Identity and location are separate objects on purpose, and this demonstration keeps them apart.
