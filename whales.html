Can a whale phrase be given an address?

A sperm whale clicks five times, and the gaps between the clicks make a rhythm. A biologist writes that rhythm down as 5R. This page asks whether a phrase like that could be given an address: a short name that says which phrases sit near which. Nothing here decodes a whale.

established contested speculative

A coda is a rhythm, and a firing neuron is a rate

A sperm whale makes short bursts of clicks called codas. One common coda is five clicks with a steady beat, and a field biologist writes it down as 5R or 1+1+3. The same note records the tempo and the gaps between the clicks. If you have ever tapped a rhythm on a table, you already have the whole idea of the first description.

The second description comes from the listening side. A neuron fires, and what a recording of it shows is a spike rate: how many times that neuron fired in a second. A neurophysiologist may also record a delay, a path through the activity of many neurons at once, or a change in blood oxygen that an MRI scanner detects.

Both descriptions are correct, and both are written in their own vocabulary. The problem is that they share no frame. A frame, on this page, means one shared set of questions: the list of questions, the exact wording of each one, and the rule that turns the answers into a name. Without a frame there is no conversion between the two descriptions, in either direction.

The two recordings are ordinary. Both have been made many times, and both are reliable. What is unusual is that nothing joins them: a coda has a whole literature behind it, and a whale brain has none of the recordings you would need to meet it. That is the question the next section answers.

The first question everybody asks is why no one records a whale brain

You cannot put a chronic implant in a wild cetacean. Ethics and permits rule it out, and so does the practical side. The animal is enormous, it dives, it is immersed in water, and its brain anatomy is unlike that of the animals whose brains are recorded routinely. So the work that exists is non-invasive.

Four other animals do have the recording this idea would need. Each row below is an established result with its own primary source, and the last row is the whale.

Two non-invasive methods do exist. Auditory evoked potentials pick up an electrical response from the surface of the animal, and behavioural psychoacoustics measures what an animal can hear and tell apart. For the question on this page, those two methods are the ceiling.

Any claim of a whale phrase-to-neuron map is speculative until a lawful, interpretable neural measurement exists. The four animals above are the whole of the evidence base.
The absence is not a gap that somebody forgot to close. It is a limit of what can be done, lawfully and safely, to a protected diving animal, and every proposal later on this page inherits it. Four animals have the measurement a mapping would need, and the animal this page is about does not.

A phrase needs two names, and they must not be the same name

We want two things that pull in opposite directions. We want to say that two recordings are nearly the same. We also want to say that two recordings are exactly the same, with no threshold to argue about. So the design keeps three separate objects, each doing one job.

An address is like a postcode. The first letters already tell you the region, and the whole thing tells you the street, so two postcodes that start the same way are near each other. Where the comparison breaks: a postcode is handed out by an authority, while this address is worked out from the phrase itself, so nobody has to agree on it in advance.

A root is a fingerprint for the exact set of statements, taken in a fixed order. Change a single statement and the fingerprint changes completely. Where that breaks as a locator: a fingerprint can say identical or different, and it can never say near.

A prediction is an estimate of a measured neural response for a phrase the fitting never saw. It is not a decoded message, and it is not an address.

The mapping is built on a 3,300-byte seed

Two sides can compare addresses only if they are using the same frame, and a frame has many parts: the questions, the wording of each question, the order they are asked in, the sound alphabet, the distance table, and the rule for a fingerprint. Sending all of that is expensive. The alternative is to send a small file and rebuild the rest.

That small file is called the seed. It is 3,300 bytes, which is about the length of a paragraph, and it is the same file the parent project uses. It stores settings and rules. Everything that follows from those rules is derived by the receiver, so it never has to be transmitted.

Eleven keys, and one of them is 70 per cent of the file. The key to look at is the smallest interesting one: it is named partition, it is 74 bytes, and it holds a rule rather than a list. Everything a registry would have to store and keep in step, this file works out again from that rule.

The seed goes out. The receiver rebuilds the frame from it, and that frame is 4,532 bytes. If the frame were shipped instead, as a registry-style exchange would have to ship it, the landmarks, the codebook and the population would cost 67,561 bytes. That is 20.47 times the seed.

The one line that decides whether you send a rule or a list

Look again at the key named partition. It holds three settings: 45 questions, four possible answers at each question, and the order descending-variance. That is a rule. Applied to the 204 places, the rule produces the order 36, 35, 38, 30, 29, 3, 26, 23, 12, 41 and continues from there.

A registry would ship that order as a list and keep it versioned forever, because a list cannot explain itself. This file ships the rule and lets both sides work out the list.

One line, 74 bytes, and both sides agree without coordinating. The rule is stored, the order is derived, and neither side has to trust the other side's copy of the order.

Now change one number and watch what it costs. Take the seed and change similarity.minhash_seed from 42 to 43. Everything else stays the same. The fingerprint of the frame changes, because the fingerprint covers every setting, including this one.

Two sides that were once using the same frame are now using different frames. The library does not guess, and it does not quietly compare addresses from two different rulebooks. It refuses, in its own words.

{ "comparable": false,
  "reason": "different primer: the two geometries are incommensurable; only exact identity can be compared",
  "same_identity": true }

In plain words: the two sides are working from different rulebooks, so the library will not compare their addresses at all. It will still compare exact identity, because identical statements give identical bytes whatever frame is in use. A wrong silent comparison is the failure this refusal prevents.

This is the behaviour a registry cannot copy. A registry stores an answer and must version it forever. This stores the rule, derives the answer, and stops rather than compares across two different frames. The refusal is a few lines of code and it is already implemented.

The bridge from a recording to a prediction, in five steps

The aim is to turn a recording into a short address, and then to ask whether that address predicts a measured neural response. Every step below is a quantity somebody can measure or a search somebody can bound, and none of it needs a whale to have a language. No step here has been run on a whale, and that stays true to the end of the page.

step 01

Turn one phrase into a list of 18 numbers

A phrase vector is a list of numbers measured from the recording. The first number is the click count, or the number of syllables in a phrase. Numbers 2 to 6 are the first five gaps between clicks, in milliseconds and also as a fraction of the phrase length, with a marker for a gap that is not there. Number 7 is the median tempo, in events per second.

Numbers 8 to 10 describe rubato, which is timing that is stretched away from a strict grid: the average stretch, how much it varies, and the largest stretch. Numbers 11 to 14 are the brightness of the sound at four points in time, where brightness means where the energy sits on the high-to-low scale. Numbers 15 and 16 are the spread of energy early and late. Numbers 17 and 18 are ornamentation, which counts extra non-rhythmic events in the sound.

Then add them up, so the reader can check the arithmetic: 1 + 5 + 1 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 2 = 18.

established these are measurable acoustic quantities. speculative that this list of 18 numbers is the biologically relevant one, because microphone placement, travel through water, overlapping calls and detector error can all be larger than the differences we care about.

step 02

Measure the phrase against 64 reference phrases

We need a fixed set of landmarks, so that any two recordings become numbers that can be compared. An anchor is a reference phrase you measure a new phrase against. The proposal is to fix 64 of them, chosen before any neural fitting. They come either from clustering a training corpus, a standard way of grouping examples into similar piles, repeated 100 times with the best split on unseen data kept, or from hand-picked examples that span click count, tempo, timing shape and ornamentation.

For each anchor we record how far away the phrase is. Continuous numbers use straight-line distance. Shapes that stretch in time use a method that lines them up before comparing. Flags use a count of how many flags differ. The distances are then sorted into four levels, in the same spirit as the four levels 0.0, 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9 in the parent frame, with the cut points set on training data.

contested no single way of measuring distance is justified in advance, and a poor set of anchors is a known failure: repeated anchors collapse together, outliers make everything look far away, and anchors drawn from one recording site can end up describing the equipment instead of the whale.

step 03

Turn the 64 distances into a short address

Sixty-four distances are still too many to compare by eye. So each distance becomes one question with four possible answers, and the questions are asked in the order that separates phrases best. The order is measured on training data only, and it is worked out rather than stored. Sixty-four questions with four answers each is 128 ticks, two for every question.

Those ticks are then written as syllables, using the same 32-syllable codebook as the parent frame. The syllables are chosen so that neighbouring answers produce neighbouring sounds. A small change in the phrase is therefore a small change in the address, and a listener can hear how far apart two phrases are without doing any arithmetic.

The order matters more here than it does in the parent demonstration. The first question asked should probably be click count or tempo rather than brightness. That is a hypothesis to test, not a result, and it is the clearest example on this page of a choice that nobody has checked yet.

step 04

Write down the prediction, and say what it would mean

The question is whether the address tells you anything about a measured neural response. In the formula below, q(x) is the address of the phrase, written as a list of numbers rather than syllables. y is the neural response, which might be firing rates counted in short windows, latency, or a path through the activity of a population of neurons. W and b are numbers the fitting is allowed to choose.

The prediction is worth taking seriously only if it beats a preregistered null on phrases that were held back, and if it still works across recording sessions, individual animals and contexts.

A fit that worked would mean the address holds information useful for predicting a measured response. It would not mean the address is a neural code, and it would not mean the animal computes anything like this frame. Nonlinear versions are tested only after this straight-line baseline, never before it.

step 05

Make the search large enough that the null has to be large too

Every choice above could be made several ways, and each way is tried. There are roughly 10³ candidate anchor sets, 6 distance measures, 5 sorting schemes, 4 question orderings and 3 ways of lining the response up with the sound. Multiply those and you get 10³ × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 = 360,000 candidate structures.

For each one, the fitting is repeated over 20 penalty strengths, which is a standard way of keeping the fitted numbers small. That gives 360,000 × 20 = 7,200,000 fitted candidates before any shuffling is added. The fitting uses nested cross-validation: the inner rounds choose anchors, measures, sorting, order and penalty, and the outer rounds then score the result on phrases and sessions that were never used for choosing.

A candidate that wins 7.2 million trials by chance has not discovered a map. The search is that large on purpose, and the null has to be that large with it.

5 steps · 18 numbers per phrase · 64 reference phrases · 128 ticks · 360,000 structures · 7,200,000 fits

Every number in these five steps is something a person can measure or count, and none of them requires a whale to have a language. The step to notice is the last: the search is 7.2 million fits wide, so a good-looking result on its own means almost nothing. That is why the next sections are about controls rather than about findings, and why this page still has no result about whales.

The pipeline, and where each control attaches

Six stages take one calibrated recording to a prediction of a response the fitting never saw. Each stage could be corrupted by a different mistake, so each stage has a control hanging off it. The phrase address is stage five and the prediction is stage six, which means nothing downstream can be scored before the address is frozen.

The address is stage five, and that placement is deliberate. Everything scored downstream depends on it, so a control that is worth anything has to freeze the address before any neural response is looked at. A pipeline that chooses its address while looking at the answer is not testing anything.

The controls, and why they are the hard part

Here is why controls exist at all. The search tries 7,200,000 fitted candidates. Faced with a choice that large, a result that looks impressive can mean nothing, because some candidate was always going to look good on the data it was chosen from. A control is the check that a result survives when the thing being tested is removed. Without controls, the search itself produces the finding.

The tools used for the checking are long established. Canonical correlation analysis finds the best straight-line link between two sets of measurements (Hotelling 1936). Orthogonal Procrustes lines two shapes up under a fixed rule (Schönemann 1966, Psychometrika, doi:10.1007/BF02289451). Representational similarity analysis compares how dissimilar things are instead of assuming two sets of coordinates mean the same thing (Kriegeskorte et al. 2008, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, doi:10.3389/neuro.01.1.1.004.2008). Models that learn to predict a neural measurement from features of the stimulus are the standard tool for this kind of question (Naselaris et al. 2011, NeuroImage, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2010.07.073), and maps of where meaning lands in the brain show both the ambition and the assumptions (Huth et al. 2016, Nature, doi:10.1038/nature17637).

One failure has its own name because it is so easy to commit by accident. Circular analysis means choosing the features because they fit the neural data, and then judging those same features on those same data (Kriegeskorte et al. 2009, Nature Neuroscience, doi:10.1038/nn.2303). The protection is simple to state: the address is frozen before the test phrases are examined, and no cleaning step is ever allowed to see a phrase it is about to be scored on.

The controls, not the model, are where this kind of work succeeds or fails. A search this wide will always produce something that looks convincing, and seven gates is what stands between that and a claim. The gates cost more work than the fitting does.

With a few phrases and thousands of neurons, a fit can be pure luck

Before naming any method, here is the trap in everyday terms. If you have enough knobs to turn, you can fit a curve to any handful of points, and the curve will touch every one of them. It will then predict nothing new. Where the analogy breaks: a fitted curve is not useless on purpose, and a real fit can genuinely work. The problem is only that touching the data you fitted is no evidence at all about data you did not.

The setting on this page is exactly that shape. A study might have a few hundred phrases and a neural response spread over thousands of neurons. When the number of measurements available to fit with approaches the number of phrases you have, a flexible method can align noise and report a near-perfect match. The method with this reputation is the one named in the section above, mentioned once, and the failure is called sample-size collapse.

Reducing the number of measurements does not fix it by itself. If the reduction is chosen using all the phrases, including the ones used to score the result, the leak has moved one step earlier.

Sample-size collapse. That method can produce a near-perfect match measured on the same data that chose the fit, when the number of measurements approaches the number of phrases. A few hundred phrases against thousands of neurons is that regime, and the search over anchors, measures, alignments and penalties makes it easier. Reducing the measurements does not cure it automatically, because a reduction chosen on all the phrases moves the leak upstream.
This is the honest state of the mapping: with the data that exists, a convincing-looking result is easy to produce and hard to trust. The controls above are the only thing that turns the difficulty into something a reader can judge, and they are why the whale settings of the plan stop early.

The real result, against each control

The numbers in this section come from the parent repository, where six independently built systems were each asked to place six places. Every system was compared with every other system for every place, which is 90 comparisons, and the same place came back nearest in all 90 of them.

Counting exact ties, that score is 100.0 per cent. Strictly, with ties counted against it, it is 96.7 per cent. Chance is 16.7 per cent, which is one in six, because there are six places to choose from.

Two nulls are the controls that matter here. Shuffling the phrase labels scores 16.9 per cent with a standard deviation of 16.8 over 6,000 trials. A random vocabulary of equal size, which replaces each system's selection with a random set of the same size drawn from the same 45 statements, scores 20.9 per cent with a standard deviation of 16.5 over the same 6,000 trials.

Replacing each system's chosen statements with random statements of the same length drops the score from 96.7 per cent to 20.9 per cent. What carries the result is which statements the systems chose, not how many they were given. The two nulls sit on top of chance and on top of each other, and only the real result separates from them.

Six settings, each with the number that would end it

Every setting below names what it measures and the single result that would end it. None of them is a whale whose neural response cannot be measured, which is why the whale settings stop where they do. These are proposals, and every one of them is a proposal.

  • 00 · synthetic phrase spaces, no whales

    Phrase spaces carry known reference phrases, a known question order, known sorting and a known W. Falsifier: the pipeline cannot recover W, or its performance on unseen phrases collapses under realistic noise. This setting is where failure is cheapest to find, so it varies the number of phrases until the sample-size collapse becomes visible.

  • 01 · bird song, where the sound and the response both exist

    Zebra-finch song numbers against auditory-forebrain responses, with familiarity of the tutor as a known contrast. Falsifier: the method fails to tell known song classes apart, or cannot beat a simple spectrogram baseline on held-out birds and sessions. This tests the whole path from recording to response where the recordings exist.

  • 02 · bat echolocation, where the physical variable is known

    Echolocation numbers against auditory-cortex single-unit responses, including delay and velocity tuning. Falsifier: a structure of this kind performs no better than shuffled addresses, or cannot recover a range-like response surface. This tests whether the reference phrases and the question order preserve a known physical variable.

  • 03 · marmoset vocal feedback

    Self-generated calls against A1 activity, in the Eliades & Wang 2008 setting. Falsifier: no prediction on held-out phrases beyond the baselines of acoustic energy, onset and motor feedback. This tests active feedback rather than a passive response to a sound.

  • 04 · cetacean behaviour, without neurons

    Stable coda and song addresses built from independently recorded populations, tested on identity, context, overlap, matching, cultural change and stability across sites. Falsifier: addresses fail to replicate when a microphone, a recorder, a location or an individual is held out. This is useful structure engineering, and it is not a neural map.

  • 05 · non-invasive cetacean physiology, if it is ever justified

    Controlled acoustic presentation paired with auditory evoked potentials or other permitted non-invasive measures. Falsifier: no reproducible phrase-conditioned response after correcting for loudness, timing, movement and subject identity. Even success would establish prediction of a measured evoked signal, not cortical meaning.

At every setting the address has to beat raw acoustic numbers, a standard spectrogram summary, a simple timing model, and a randomly generated structure carrying the same amount of information. That comparison is the whole test of whether this structure earns its extra complexity.

6 settings · 1 falsifier each · 4 baselines to beat at every setting

Nothing here has been run. The first setting is the one that would end the idea most cheaply, and it uses no animals at all. A plan whose cheapest test can kill it, and whose hardest tests are listed as clearly as the easy ones, is the most this page can honestly offer.

What this cannot become

Nothing in this proposal can establish that a coda or a song phrase has meaning. A predictive acoustic-to-neural map would not identify a referent, an intention or a semantic composition. It would be a model that takes a signal in and gives a measured response out, and a good predictor is not a dictionary.

An address, a root or a neural coordinate is descriptive and never authoritative. In a whale application, a highly repeatable acoustic feature must not be promoted to a behavioural statement without independent evidence, and an inferred neural association stays a hypothesis until it survives held-out subjects, temporal shuffles, decoys and search-corrected nulls.

The strongest defensible outcome is modest: a reproducible, structure-preserving description of vocal signals, plus a rigorously tested prediction of a particular neural measurement where such data exist. For cetaceans today the first object is feasible and the second is not established.

The word doing the most work on this page is speculative. The address is real and measured, in another project and on other subjects. Everything about whales here is a proposal, and the honest summary of the whale work is that it has produced nothing yet and that the plainest possible test would say so quickly.

The marker ladder, unchanged

Every claim in the parent frame carries a marker, and a claim can only be as strong as the weakest marker in it. A model's confidence cannot promote a marker; only evidence can. The two highest rungs anchor a claim, and the other three never do.

What the demonstration contributes, and what it does not

The parent repository has a real result of its own, and it is worth separating from the whale proposal. Six independently built systems, given the same 45 statements, placed the same six situations in the same part of their own space. A question-asking generator can name any of 204 places, and it takes 17.8 questions on average, against a floor of 7.672, which is the logarithm to base two of 204.

Why six independent systems could agree at all

Handing six systems the same 45 statements does not explain the result. This argument belongs to the parent project, and this page restates it because the whale proposal depends on it.

A shared vocabulary tells you what to answer with. It does not make two differently built systems put the same situation in the same part of their own space. If a shared vocabulary were all that was happening, the addresses would be arbitrary but consistent, like two people agreeing to call a colour seven.

What the agreement implies is stronger. Situations appear to occupy a structure that independently built systems have in common. The shape of all possible situations, where nearness means likeness, is what this project calls the manifold, and the primer is a chart laid over it rather than a substitute for it.

The two halves are different kinds of object. The manifold is shared, emergent and not ours: we did not build it, we cannot inspect it directly, and its existence is inferred from the fact that independent systems agree. The chart is ours, published and small: 45 statements, a 32-syllable codebook, a question order derived over 204 places, one hashing rule, 3,300 bytes.

the manifold is inferred from agreement · the chart is published and 3,300 bytes

Six systems built by different organisations, on different data, with no shared weights and no sight of each other, put the same situation in the same part of their own space in 87 of 90 comparisons. The obvious explanation, that they were handed the same questions, does not survive contact with the numbers. Something about situations is common to systems that have nothing else in common.

The strongest thing the numbers license is a way of describing where a situation sits, and the weakest thing they license is meaning. The gap between those two is the whole reason the whale section of this page stays a proposal.

The shared-vocabulary caveat, and nothing here removes it

The shared-vocabulary caveat is inherited, not discovered here. The parent experiment's handover report states it first: its blind cross-model matching used a shared anchor vocabulary and a rule that gives the same output for the same input every time, so it demonstrates matching at the level of turning answers into an address, given a shared primer. It does not establish independence from a shared ontology. Its recommended follow-up, a genuine-versus-shuffled-anchors-versus-random-loci-versus-garbage study, has still not been run.

The parent project also set out the falsifier. Independence from a shared ontology is shown only when genuine anchors beat shuffled anchors, structure-valid random places, and an empty or garbage condition. It is not shown by a run with shared labels scoring well. This repository restates the caveat because it still applies and nothing here removes it.

That narrows the caveat without removing it. The systems still chose from a vocabulary we handed them, so what is shown is that the frame is usable by systems with nothing in common, not that systems with different vocabularies would invent the same one.

They agreed on 88.8 per cent of their statement choices, and the addresses inherit that agreement. The warehouse alone is the worst case: five of the six systems produced five different statement sets and five different roots, and only two landed on the same root. The locked storeroom and the dark empty room are the best, at five of six each.

No claim is made about meaning, understanding or intent. The address is descriptive and never authoritative, and identity and location are separate objects on purpose.
